Most first impressions are not arguments. They are assessments of care. Before a visitor reads your positioning statement, they have already registered whether the page loads with a plan, whether the type feels intentional, and whether anything is moving for a reason.

Three seconds is not much time. It is enough to answer three questions.

## 1. What is this?

Not what does the company do — that comes second. First: what kind of thing am I looking at? A product, a studio, a publication, a place? The answer comes from typography, density, rhythm and the first image far more than from a tagline.

We design the opening frame so that the answer is visible before it is readable. A clear scale relationship between the headline, the supporting line and the navigation does most of the work. When everything is loud, the answer becomes "noise" — and the visitor leaves before the sentence finishes.

## 2. Is it for me?

Belonging is a design problem. The visitor is scanning for evidence that the site was made with someone like them in mind: the vocabulary, the examples, the level of technical specificity, the absence of generic stock imagery.

This is where restraint earns its keep. A page that shows three real things with confidence is more persuasive than a page that shows twelve things vaguely. If the work is not ready to be shown, say so. A clearly labelled sample is more honest than a borrowed photograph.

> A first screen should feel like a door opening, not a crowd shouting.

## 3. Can I trust where this goes?

Trust is built by small consistencies. A visible focus state. A link that goes where it says. A form that admits when it has not sent. A phone layout that does not overflow. None of these are memorable individually. Together they answer a quieter question: if I invest five more minutes here, will I be taken care of?

## The motion question

Cinematic does not mean constant. We use motion for three jobs:

- **Orientation** — showing where something came from, or where it went.
- **Hierarchy** — letting the eye finish one idea before starting the next.
- **Character** — a small, recognisable behaviour that belongs to the brand.

Anything else is usually decoration with a performance cost. That is why our reveals are short, our page transitions are quicker than they feel, and the whole experience survives with animation turned off. The content is in the HTML. The motion is applied on top.

## A practical checklist

1. Can the page be understood in a static screenshot?
2. Does the largest type say the most important thing?
3. Is the primary action visible without scrolling on a laptop and a phone?
4. Does the first load work on a slow connection, with images still arriving?
5. Does the page still make sense with JavaScript disabled, or with reduced motion enabled?

If the answer to those is yes, the first three seconds have done their job. The next fifty-seven can be about the work.
